Summary

Two questions, two different answers

You've been quoted $550,000 and a fast sale. Those are two separate claims, and the market data supports them very differently.

On price, $550,000 is reachable but sits near the top of the range. Adjusted against the three Split Oak Estates properties that closed this year, this investment reconciles to a value most probably near $555,000. The number you've been given is not unreasonable — but it is closer to a ceiling than a starting point, and reaching it will take time.

On timing, the data does not support 30 days. Not at $550,000, and not at any price. Every Split Oak property that closed above $500,000 this year took between 269 and 293 days to find a buyer. That is the constraint no pricing strategy can remove, and it deserves to be understood before you make a decision.

You can have $550,000, or you can have speed. The good news is that in this particular market, the gap between them is much smaller than you'd expect.

The market that used to move quickly

One property shows the shift on its own record. 5647 Barletta went under contract in 32 days in October 2022 and closed at $595,000. The same property returned to the market in March 2025 at $629,000, required nine price reductions across 293 days, and closed at $523,000 in January 2026.

Same house, same community, same street: a $72,000 decline and nine times the marketing period. Fast sales in Split Oak are not impossible — they were routine four years ago. They are simply not what this market is producing now.

The Cause

Why these properties are sitting

Long marketing times in Split Oak are not a reflection of the properties. All of the comparables are attractive, well-kept investments in a desirable community, and so is yours. Something structural is holding them on the market, and it is worth understanding before you decide to join them.

Your competition is not the other resale listings. It is the builders — and they are competing with a tool you do not have.

What a rate buydown is actually worth

Builders are offering interest rate buydowns, closing cost credits, and included upgrades. In March 2026 the National Association of Home Builders reported that 64% of builders were running sales incentives of some kind. Those incentives do not appear in any price comparison, because they are not price. They are payment — and a financed buyer shops payment.

The 30-year fixed rate averaged 6.58% nationally for the week of July 23, 2026, per Freddie Mac's survey. Builder buydowns commonly land between 4.5% and 5.5%. Applied to a buyer putting 20% down on a $550,000 purchase:

Same $550,000 purchaseRateMonthly P&IEquivalent price at market rate
Resale, market financing6.58%$2,804—
New build, moderate buydown5.50%$2,498~$490,000
New build, aggressive buydown4.99%$2,360~$463,000

Read the last column as what a resale seller would have to price at to deliver the builder's monthly payment using price alone. The buydown is worth somewhere between $60,000 and $87,000 in purchase price to that buyer — and it costs the builder considerably less than that to provide.

Two honest qualifications. Many builder buydowns are temporary rather than permanent — a common structure reduces the rate for the first two years and then reverts to the full note rate, which means the payment relief has an expiration date the buyer may not be weighing. And the deepest buydowns typically require using the builder's affiliated lender. Both are genuine counterpoints a listing agent can raise. Neither changes the fact that the buyer is comparing first-year payments, which is precisely why builders structure incentives this way.

This is the whole explanation. It is why 5647 Barletta required nine price reductions across 293 days. It is why 2768 Matera cycled through three listings and two and a half years. Those sellers were not mispriced by a little. They were competing against an incentive that price cannot match, and each reduction simply moved them closer to a line they were never going to reach.

You cannot outprice a rate buydown. The sellers in this data spent nine months discovering that. The reductions in their listing histories are what that discovery looks like.

What we have watched this produce

This is not a forecast. It is a pattern we now see repeatedly, and it resolves the same way. A comparable investment in Isle of Lake Nona sat on the sale market for 222 days through exactly this dynamic. The owner eventually stopped, and we leased it in two days.

That gap — 222 days to sell, two days to lease — is not a statement about that particular property. It is a statement about where the demand currently is. The buyer pool at this price point is being absorbed by builders. The tenant pool is not.

The Real Math

What waiting actually costs

Holding this investment vacant and on the market, rather than leased, costs roughly $3,800 to $4,200 a month. That figure has two halves worth separating. About $2,595 of it is rent you are not collecting — money that does not arrive. The remaining $1,200 to $1,600 is cash actually leaving your account each month: non-homesteaded taxes near $600, HOA dues, insurance at vacancy rates, and the utilities and lawn care needed to keep the property presentable for showings.

Your mortgage sits outside that figure, but it does not sit outside this decision. It splits in two, and the halves behave very differently. The principal portion of each payment is not a cost at all — it reduces your loan balance, so it returns to you at closing as a smaller payoff. You are moving money from one pocket to another. The interest portion is gone permanently, and because each path below reaches the closing table at a different time, the three do not owe the same amount of it.

The comparison below therefore carries a separate line for interest, illustrated at roughly $810 per month — approximately the interest on a $300,000 balance at a 2020-era rate. Replace it with the figure from your own mortgage statement. The shape of the answer will not change, but the size will.

The most expensive path is the one that looks like patience. Chasing the top of the range costs money rather than earning it.

Enter your interest, not your principal

The principal portion of your mortgage payment does not belong anywhere on this page. It reduces your loan balance, which means it comes back to you at closing as a smaller payoff. You are moving money between your own pockets, not spending it. Carrying Cost

What a mortgage payment actually costs you

It is natural to think of every month of carrying as money coming off your sale price. That is close to right, but not exactly right, and the difference is worth several thousand dollars in either direction. A payment splits into money you get back and money you do not.

On a mortgage originated in 2020, a larger share of each payment is principal than most owners assume, because a low interest rate amortizes faster. Your true monthly cost of holding is therefore lower than your payment — but everything below that first line is genuinely spent, and no eventual sale price recovers it. It is not deferred. It is not recouped. It reduces whatever you finally net, dollar for dollar.

The comparison that matters most

The real question is not whether holding costs money. It is which version of holding you are doing.

  • Vacant and listed: full carrying cost, zero income. Roughly $3,800–$4,200 per month against you.
  • Leased at $2,595: income covers the great majority of that cost. Depending on your payment, likely somewhere between modestly negative and modestly positive.

Those two states differ by roughly a factor of ten. Whatever you decide about selling, the expensive condition is an empty property, and every month it sits empty is the most costly month available to you.

The rate is an asset, and selling ends it

Your instinct that you will not see this financing again is not sentiment — it is arithmetic. The gap between your rate and today's market rate is a benefit you collect every month for as long as you hold the loan. As a rule of thumb, each percentage point of difference on a $300,000 balance is worth roughly $3,000 a year. A three-point gap is close to $9,000 annually, for as long as the loan runs.

Selling converts that into nothing. It is the one asset here that cannot be rebought at any price, and it belongs on the ledger next to the sale proceeds — not left out of the comparison because it does not appear on a closing statement.

Before Listing

Two numbers to settle in advance

The most expensive outcome available to you is not selling low, and it is not holding too long. It is doing both: listing high, reducing repeatedly, spending six or seven months and $25,000 or more in carrying cost, and then leasing the property anyway — having surrendered the price and the money and gained nothing.

That is not a hypothetical. It is the most common pattern in this data. 5647 Barletta took nine price reductions across 293 days. 2768 Matera cycled through three separate listings and two and a half years. Neither seller planned that. Each simply never decided in advance where the line was, and the market decided for them, one reduction at a time.

Decision point

Decide these before the sign goes in the yard — and write them down

1
Your floor price. The number below which you will not sell, chosen while you are calm rather than in month five. If the market will not meet it, you lease. Not a target — a floor.
2
Your stop date. The date on which, if the property is not under contract, you stop marketing and lease it. Given the Split Oak evidence, any date inside 90 days will almost certainly be reached, and any date beyond 180 days should be chosen deliberately with the carrying cost calculated in advance, not discovered along the way.

Write both down now. Every month past your stop date costs roughly $4,000 that no future sale price returns to you.

There is a third question worth answering honestly alongside those two: how long can you comfortably carry a mortgage in Osceola County while also paying rent in South Florida? Not how long is theoretically possible — how long before it becomes a strain that affects your decisions. That answer should set your stop date, rather than the other way around.

Third Option

The door that stays open

Lease now, revisit the sale in twelve months

The investment is rent-ready today and supports $2,595 per month. A twelve-month lease produces roughly $31,000 in gross rent and eliminates the carrying drag entirely, instead of absorbing $12,000 to $30,000 of it.

It also addresses the financing obstacle directly. Lenders that decline to credit projected rental income will frequently credit documented rental income — a signed lease with a payment history behind it. Twelve months of landlord history is often precisely the qualification that unlocks the next purchase.

The honest cost: you cannot list effectively while a tenant occupies the property. Showings become restricted, the buyer pool narrows toward investors, and investors pay less. Choosing this path means committing to it for the lease term. It is also possible that builder incentives persist or deepen into next year, in which case the sale market may not be meaningfully better twelve months from now.

The part that is genuinely time-sensitive

Leasing has a season, and it is the one we are in. Central Florida's rental demand peaks through the summer, driven by relocations and families moving before the school year. Late July is still inside that window, but it is closing. Demand thins through the fall and reaches its slowest point between November and January.

This matters because of the sequence you are considering. If the property lists in August and follows the pattern in this analysis, it will not be under contract before the leasing window has closed. A decision to stop and lease would then land in December or January — the weakest four weeks of the year for this — where the same investment takes materially longer to place and typically requires a rent concession to move at all.

Before You Decide

One conversation worth having first

Verify the mortgage constraint independently

The guidance that you cannot obtain financing while owning and leasing this investment is frequently a specific lender's internal overlay rather than a universal underwriting rule. Conventional guidelines commonly permit documented lease income to offset a departing residence, subject to conditions that vary considerably between lenders. Builder-affiliated lenders are typically among the most restrictive.

Before selling a 2020 investment property in a soft window, it is worth a second opinion from an independent lender with no relationship to the builder. At $2,595 per month in lease income, the debt-to-income arithmetic may look materially different than you have been told. That single conversation could change which of these paths makes sense — or make the question unnecessary.

Verandah does not originate mortgages and this is not lending advice. It is simply a recommendation to get a second read before an irreversible decision.

In Short

The bottom line

  • $550,000 is close to the top of the range, not the middle of it. The value reconciles to $545,000–$570,000, most probably near $555,000, with a hard appraisal ceiling around $570,000.
  • Thirty days is not achievable at any price. Every Split Oak sale above $500,000 this year took between 269 and 293 days. There were no exceptions.
  • Chasing the top of the range loses money. Once holding costs and interest are counted, the patient path finishes behind the balanced one despite a higher sale price. Speed costs about $5,500 and buys a defined end date.
  • Your competition is the builders, not the neighbors. A rate buydown is worth $60,000 to $87,000 in price to a financed buyer. No price reduction you make can match it.
  • An empty property is the expensive one. Vacant and listed runs roughly $4,000 per month against you. Leased, that figure comes close to zero.
  • Your financing is an asset with real value that selling permanently extinguishes, and it belongs in the comparison.
  • The leasing window is open now and closes in the fall. A sale attempt that fails puts you in the winter rental market, which costs roughly $24,000 you cannot recover.
  • Set a floor price and a stop date before listing. The costliest outcome is reducing for six months and leasing anyway.

Whichever direction you choose, we would rather you choose it with an accurate picture than an encouraging one. If the decision is to sell, we will release the management agreement cleanly and without friction, and we are glad to coordinate the handoff so nothing falls between the two processes.
